# Keep the Argentine voice, verify every clip

**Stay on ElevenLabs for both jobs, but change how you use it, and line up Azure's Argentine voices as the tested backup.** On ElevenLabs the accent comes from the voice, not the model, so an Argentine voice on the top-ranked real-time model (`eleven_v4_turbo`) is still the best live setup for Pancho that the research could find. For course audio, use the same voice on a non-real-time model and check every clip automatically (transcribe it back, check its length, retry, then let a native listen to the single words). No provider publishes error rates for single Spanish words, and no one has published a native-ear test of any Argentine voice, so the big open questions can only be closed by a small listening test of your own. Three risks need action now: "Agustín" is a library voice its owner can withdraw; the v4 launch discount (72% off) ends **October 12, 2026**, after which live chat costs about 3.6 times more per character; and your slow mode sits close to ElevenLabs' speed floor of 0.7, which is the likely reason it sounds metallic. The discount is worth only tens of dollars on a full course run, so it is not a reason to rush a regeneration you have not tested.

## The accent lives in the voice, and only four vendors sell one

ElevenLabs has no Argentine model or setting. Its v4 models list Spanish only as "Spanish, LatAm", and the docs say that when the text language matches the voice's own language, "the original accent is preserved" ([ElevenLabs models](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/models), [Eleven v4 docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/text-to-speech/eleven-v4)). So Agustín sounds porteño because the speaker is porteño. The `language_code` field only forces the language and the number reading; it cannot say "Argentina", and **Multilingual v2 ignores it completely** ([TTS stream reference](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/stream)). That fits what you heard: v2 is the older, flatter model, and it is also the one that cannot be told "this lone word is Spanish".

Outside ElevenLabs, ready-made Argentine voices are rare. **Azure has two** (`es-AR-ElenaNeural`, `es-AR-TomasNeural`), both on its older, classic engine with no speaking styles and no HD version ([Azure language support](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/language-support?tabs=tts)). **Rime Coda has three** (abril, cielo, solsticio) ([Rime Coda voices](https://docs.rime.ai/docs/voices-coda)). **Deepgram Aura-2 has one** (antonia) ([Deepgram voices](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/tts-models)). Google's new Gemini 3.8 TTS reportedly has 53 voices tagged "Argentina Spanish", but that comes from one developer's notes, not from Google ([unicef/adt-studio #926](https://github.com/unicef/adt-studio/issues/926)). Amazon Polly has no Argentine voice at all ([Polly voices](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/polly/latest/dg/available-voices.html)). OpenAI's voices are "optimized for English" and an accent is only a written request the model can ignore ([OpenAI TTS guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/text-to-speech)). Cartesia leads most of the new multilingual quality boards, but its documented Spanish accents are Mexican, Colombian and Castilian, so Argentine there means cloning a real speaker ([Cartesia changelog](https://docs.cartesia.ai/changelog/2026), [Artificial Analysis leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/leaderboard)).

One caution covers this whole section: **every claim about how Argentine a voice sounds is marketing copy.** The research found no independent test of "ll/y" as "sh", the soft "s", or voseo stress ("tenés", "querés") for any engine.

## Your four known problems each have a documented cause

**Word swaps ("gustaría" for "gusta").** All the new expressive models pick their output by sampling, so they sometimes say something that is not in the text. ElevenLabs says its `seed` gives a "best effort" and that "determinism is not guaranteed", and its fix for bad audio is simply to regenerate ([TTS stream reference](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/stream), [troubleshooting](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/troubleshooting)). Amazon is blunter about its own generative voices: "Do not assume complete protection against hallucinated speech output" ([Polly generative voices](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/polly/latest/dg/generative-voices.html)). On full Spanish sentences the best models get about 1 word in 100 wrong ([Gradium benchmark](https://gradium.ai/content/tts-wer-benchmark-2026)). Single words are worse in theory: research on this model family says that for very short inputs they "usually synthesize words that don't exist in the input" ([RALL-E paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.03204)). No vendor publishes a single-word number. Only classic engines such as Azure Neural avoid this, because they turn text into fixed sounds before any audio is made ([Azure custom voice overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/custom-neural-voice)). In live chat you cannot check a clip before it plays, so the swap rate can be lowered but not removed.

**Metallic slow mode.** ElevenLabs speed runs from 0.7 to 1.2 and the docs warn that "extreme values may affect the quality" ([best practices](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/text-to-speech/best-practices)). Your beginner speed of 0.75 is almost at the floor. Worse, the docs disagree on whether speed even works on v4: one page says yes, the v4 page says "Style and Speed sliders are not available" ([Eleven v4 docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/text-to-speech/eleven-v4)). The learning research points the same way as your ears. Stretched audio does not help much; pauses and naturally careful speech do (next section). No source compares how any provider sounds at 0.7–0.8 in Spanish.

**Questions and exclamations.** No source at all covers how these models handle ¿? and ¡!. What the docs do say is that "punctuation significantly affects delivery in v4" and that short phrases alone "can sometimes sound abrupt or inconsistent" ([best practices](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/text-to-speech/best-practices), [Voice Design docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/voices/voice-design)). Pancho's sentences are sent one by one, so each one arrives without its neighbours. The `previous_text` and `next_text` fields exist to give that context without speaking it ([TTS convert reference](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/convert)). Whether they fix question melody is untested.

**Low stability glitches.** Stability sets "the randomness between each generation", and the Style setting above 0 is documented to cause "mispronunciation" and extra sounds ([troubleshooting](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/troubleshooting)). Your 0.6 with style at 0 is a sound place to be.

## Big apps use synthetic voices, and research backs slow-by-pauses

Duolingo's lesson audio is synthetic. It moved to Amazon Polly in 2017 after six A/B tests, then built its character voices with Microsoft's custom voice service from about **6,000 recorded sentences** per actor ([AWS blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/powering-language-learning-on-duolingo-with-amazon-polly), [Microsoft blog](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/startups/blog/duolingo-makes-learning-language-fun-with-help-from-ai/)). It runs every clip through its own speech-recognition models and built tools "to audit and correct" ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/world-character-visemes/)). Its live Video Call uses OpenAI's voice model, as do Speak's live roleplays; Speak admits such speech-to-speech models "aren't as good as text models on instruction following" ([Speak blog](https://www.speak.com/blog/live-roleplays)). The pipeline you already have (text model, then streaming TTS) is what Praktika runs with ElevenLabs, reporting 15% longer sessions after its voice upgrade ([ElevenLabs case study](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/praktika-scales-immersive-language-learning-with-elevenlabs-tts)). Talkpal moved to Inworld and reports a 40% cost cut ([Inworld](https://inworld.ai/resources/best-voice-ai-for-language-learning-apps)). Babbel still sells lessons "voiced by native speakers" ([Babbel](https://www.babbel.com/how-babbel-works)). **No major app was found with a real rioplatense course**, so there is no one to copy and no one to beat.

On learning, good TTS holds up. In a controlled study, learners understood a story equally well from a TTS voice and a human (4.57 vs 4.74 out of 6, no significant difference); the only loss was in how natural the voice was rated ([Bione & Cardoso 2020](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/54f66cfe-2240-41a9-8c61-54a2396388fa/content)). Those studies used older, worse voices than today's. There is no study on Spanish learners, on regional accents, or on long-term word memory from TTS.

On speed, the message is "a bit slower, with pauses". Fast speech (around 200 words a minute) clearly hurts lower-level learners, moderate slowing helps, and very slow adds nothing: slowing from 116 to 98 words a minute helped, slowing to 58 did not ([Chiu & Chen 2023](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/aefa/12304504e1b362d5b1e04d98f131af445cb3.pdf)). Three-second pauses at phrase breaks improved understanding while machine-slowed audio did not ([Blau](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED340234.pdf)). A 2025 brain study with Spanish speakers found that naturally careful speech improved understanding, while normal speech stretched to the same pace changed only how the sound was processed ([Piazza et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12319891/)). The CEFR itself gives no numbers; it asks for "very slow and carefully articulated, with long pauses" at A1 and "normal speed" by B2 ([CEFR scales](https://api.macmillanenglish.com/fileadmin/user_upload/Blog_and_Resources/Blogs_and_articles/CEFR-all-scales-and-all-skills.pdf)). These studies are on English, so treat the exact rates as a guide only.

On voice variety, the gain is small and comes late. A 2025 review of 79 studies found that more voices helped higher-level learners, not beginners ([SSLA meta-analysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/high-variability-phonetic-training-hvpt-a-metaanalysis-of-l2-perceptual-training-studies/6ABB8C1F32D88D53EA8D05A4565E76F6)). For new words, how often learners hear the word matters more than how many voices say it, though varied voices did improve word stress ([Uchihara et al. 2021](https://www.paveltrofimovich.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Uchihara_et_al_2021.pdf)). One good, correct voice beats several shaky ones; a second voice is a later, cheap upgrade.

## Good habits matter more than the vendor

**Clean the text before it is spoken.** Write every number, symbol and abbreviation as Spanish words ("once", not "11"). ElevenLabs gives this advice itself, and its own pages disagree about whether Flash v2.5 reads numbers correctly by default ([troubleshooting](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/troubleshooting), [models](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/models)). Send `language_code: "es"` on every request, so that words like "no", "final" or "once" are not read as English. Always write both question marks and both exclamation marks. Have Claude write short sentences with commas at natural breaks for low levels, because on v4 pauses come from punctuation, not from break tags.

**Fix repeat offenders with a dictionary.** ElevenLabs allows up to three pronunciation dictionaries per request. On Turbo and Flash these are respelling rules only; real phoneme (IPA) rules for Spanish need `eleven_v4` ([pronunciation dictionaries guide](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-api/guides/how-to/text-to-speech/pronunciation-dictionaries)). Azure's classic voices accept full SSML with phonemes and a lexicon.

**Check every stored clip, and retry.** The standard method is to transcribe each clip back to text and compare. A June 2026 paper found that making a few takes and keeping one that passes removed all severe failures (silence, early stop, repeated or invented words) at two to four takes ([arXiv 2606.18323](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18323)). Add a length check per character; you already have one for clips that are too short. ElevenLabs also has a Forced Alignment endpoint that returns a per-word "loss" score against your text ([forced alignment reference](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/forced-alignment/create)). Know the limit: transcription gives false alarms on 21–34% of pronunciation checks and misses errors that still transcribe correctly, and it cannot judge accent (vendor article, [Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/learn/per-new-standard-measuring-tts-accuracy)). So a native speaker must still listen to the single words and to everything flagged.

**Use different models for live and batch, with one voice.** Live needs speed, so use the real-time model over plain HTTP streaming, one sentence per request; ElevenLabs says websockets "may not be the best choice when the entire input text is available upfront", and on a websocket v4 Turbo waits for about 40 characters before it starts ([websocket reference](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/v-1-text-to-speech-voice-id-stream-input), [oguzhan.co review](https://www.oguzhan.co/elevenlabs-v4-review-best-practices/)). Batch has no time limit, so use the most accurate model, make several takes, and store the winner. Cache common live lines (greetings, set corrections) so they are checked once and cost nothing after.

**Set speed by level, gently.** Keep the model's speed setting between about 0.85 and 1.0 and get the rest of "slow" from shorter sentences and pauses. For course audio, store a separate slow take instead of stretching the normal one in the player.

## Conclusion

The search for "the best TTS" turns out to be the wrong question for Posta. No vendor sells a guaranteed-correct, natural, slow, Argentine voice; the modern voices sound alive but sometimes misspeak, and the exact ones sound a little flat. What separates a good learning app from a risky one is the layer around the model: clean text, context for each sentence, automatic checks with retries, and a native ear on the single words. Duolingo's own history shows the same pattern, with the voice vendor changing more than once while the checking tools stayed.

The second shift is about ownership. Posta's product is an accent, and today that accent is rented from a stranger's library voice. Since no competitor ships rioplatense at all, a voice Posta owns, recorded from a real Argentine speaker and portable between vendors, is both the main safety step and the hardest thing for anyone else to copy.
