# Measure faithfulness first, then stream Scribe

Keep ElevenLabs Scribe v2, keep tap-to-talk, and move to Scribe v2 Realtime with manual commit only after one test: does the transcript keep learner mistakes? That test matters more than the lost second. In three studies on learner English, Whisper rewrote about a quarter to a half of learner grammar errors into correct text, and it did so while having the best word error rate (WER). Nobody has measured this for Spanish, for voseo, or for Scribe, OpenAI, Deepgram, AssemblyAI or Soniox. So Posta's correction card may already be wrong in two ways: it can miss a real mistake ("yo tiene" heard as "yo tengo"), and it can invent one ("andás" written as "andas"). Streaming should save roughly 0.5 to 1.5 s per turn, but Scribe v2 Realtime is a different model from batch Scribe v2, so its faithfulness must be measured too. On UX, the market has already landed where Posta is: a manual "I'm done" tap, the transcript shown apart from the correction, and typing as an equal option. What Posta lacks is a way for the learner to check or redo a misheard line, and a rule for words no engine can hear reliably.

## Speech models fix learner mistakes, and the best WER fixes the most

Modern speech-to-text has a strong built-in language model. It prefers fluent, correct sentences, so when a learner says something wrong, the model often writes the right form instead. This is measured, but only for learner English.

In 85 hours of Swiss children speaking English, **Whisper got 45–47% of the error-marked words wrong** (a metric called WEPR, the word error rate counted only on words marked as learner errors). A model fine-tuned on learner speech got 38%. On the 20 most common errors, Whisper kept 67% as spoken and actively corrected 26%; the fine-tuned model kept 83%. The clearest case is subject–verb agreement: when a learner said "have" for "has", **Whisper wrote "has" 23% of the time** ([Michot et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03235)). That is the English twin of "yo tiene" → "yo tengo".

Two more studies agree. With Japanese adults speaking English, 99% of Whisper's noun-ending mismatches and 86% of its verb-ending mismatches were the model correcting the learner; Google and Rev AI were at 38–58% ([Koizumi 2024](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/letcj/35/0/35_15/_pdf)). On 137 sentences with planted grammar errors, Whisper wrote what was really said in only 46% of them, against 85% for a recogniser whose language model included the expected wrong forms ([arXiv 2410.01579](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01579v1)).

**Best WER does not mean best error preservation.** Whisper had the best WER in the Swiss study and the worst error preservation. A 2026 paper says commercial systems "are optimised for aggregate WER" and give no control over this trade-off ([Beyond WER](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20828)). Public leaderboards therefore reward the behaviour that hurts a correction feature.

No setting turns this off. "Verbatim" options, including Scribe's default, only control fillers and false starts; none is documented to affect grammar ([ElevenLabs STT docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/speech-to-text)). Temperature 0 lowers randomness, but whether it changes error-fixing is untested (inference). Confidence scores help only a little: Whisper gives confidence above 0.7 to 10–20% of wrong tokens in noise ([arXiv 2509.07195](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.07195)), and a "fixed" word can score high because the language model likes it.

### What is unknown for Spanish

**No study in any language other than English measures grammar-error preservation.** There is nothing for Spanish, voseo, Scribe v2, gpt-transcribe, Deepgram, AssemblyAI or Soniox. The one learner-Spanish study measures plain accuracy: English-speaking students at B2–C1, reading 4–7 s sentences, got **11–12% WER** with Whisper large-v3 and SeamlessM4T ([Sarymsakova & Martín-Rodilla 2025](https://revistes.ub.edu/index.php/phonica/article/download/49237/44105/148972)). In English, A2 learners get about twice the WER of C1 learners ([arXiv 2510.10738](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10738)). So a fair planning guess for Posta's beginners is one wrong word in every 5 to 8. That is an estimate, not a measurement.

The rest of this section is inference from those results. "Tiene" and "tengo" sound clearly different, so if "tengo" appears after the learner said "tiene", the language model did it. That makes it the cleanest test item. "Andas" and "andás" differ only in stress. The same Spanish study found that Whisper "failed to account for the duration of the accented vowel" in learner speech. A learner with flat, English-style stress gives the model almost nothing to hear, so the model picks by context. **For stress-only pairs, no engine can be trusted, and the written accent shows the model's guess, not the learner's stress.** Posta's "andas" problem is probably this, and a better engine will not fully fix it.

Keyterms are a second risk. They are built to pull the transcript toward the listed words ([ElevenLabs keyterm prompting](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-api/guides/how-to/speech-to-text/batch/keyterm-prompting)). AssemblyAI warns that many keyterms, especially common words, "could lead to overcorrections and hallucinations" ([AssemblyAI docs](https://www.assemblyai.com/docs/streaming/prompting-and-keyterms)), and Deepgram says not to boost common words ([Deepgram keyterm docs](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/keyterm)). Posta's voseo keyterms (tenés, querés, sos) likely help when the learner really says voseo. They can also make the engine write "tenés" when the learner said "tienes" or "tiene". No vendor publishes a false-positive rate, so this needs an A/B test.

## No provider proves faithfulness, so pick on controls and test

On clean read Spanish (FLEURS), every serious model scores 2–3.5% WER, so that benchmark cannot separate them. On Common Voice Spanish, which is closer to phone recordings, the spread is four times wider: Voxtral Mini Transcribe V2 3.9%, GPT-4o mini Transcribe 5.5%, Whisper 5.7%, and **Scribe v2 Realtime 15.9%**. That last number comes from a competitor's paper and may reflect how they drove the API, but it is a warning for short, noisy clips ([Voxtral Realtime paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.11298)). Batch Scribe v2 was not in that table. No provider publishes numbers for learner Spanish or for 2–15 s lines.

Three things follow from the table. First, **price does not decide this**: a 10 s line costs about $0.0006 on batch Scribe and $0.0011 on Scribe Realtime. Second, per-word confidence is the control Posta most needs for "which word should I doubt", and Scribe Realtime and Soniox document it while OpenAI's new models give none. Third, the es-AR locale probably matters little: only Azure has one, the learners' accent is English, and nothing shows a locale code changes voseo output (inference).

Silence and one-word lines are a shared weak spot. Whisper-family models invent stock phrases on near-silent 2–3 s clips ([whisper discussion #1606](https://github.com/openai/whisper/discussions/1606)). OpenAI's newer models instead echo the prompt back on no-speech audio ([OpenAI community thread](https://community.openai.com/t/gpt-4o-transcribe-why-does-the-final-output-sometimes-exactly-replicate-the-configured-prompt/1226877/3)). No data exists for Scribe, or for any provider on "sí", "no" or "dale". Posta's silence filter is the right guard and should stay.

## Learner apps settled on a manual stop and a visible transcript

**Being cut off while thinking is the best-documented failure of open-mic voice for learners.** Duolingo's Video Call interrupted users so often that Duolingo added a button: tap to start, tap again when done ([duoplanet](https://duoplanet.com/duolingo-video-call/)). ChatGPT users learning languages make the same complaint and ask for hold-to-talk back ([OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/chatgpt-live-voice-still-interupts-too-much-what-happened-to-hold-to-talk/1386172)). Langua says auto-send is "generally best left off", because "if you pause whilst thinking, it may send before you're ready" ([Langua help guide](https://support.languatalk.com/article/160-learn-how-to-use-langua-effectively-conversations-help-guide)). The best turn detectors still cut off 4.5–10% of fluent speakers ([LiveKit](https://livekit.com/blog/solving-end-of-turn-detection)), and no vendor publishes numbers for hesitant learners. Posta's tap-to-start, tap-to-stop is the right default. Do not replace it with an open mic.

The apps with the longest speech history (Speak, ELSA, Babbel, Duolingo) say they run their own models trained on learner speech. Speak cut WER by more than 60% by training on its own learner audio, and it uses different speech paths for drills, open talk and live roleplay ([Speak](https://www.speak.com/blog/asr-levelup)). Praktika runs a tuned Whisper and got transcription under 300 ms, down from 1,000–1,500 ms ([Baseten](https://www.baseten.co/resources/customers/praktika/)). No app says whether its transcript is verbatim, and none shows uncertain words to the learner.

Live partial text is proven for scripted drills: Speak updates word feedback at least every 260 ms. For open chat it is optional. On feedback, the common pattern matches Posta's card: keep the learner's message as said and put the correction under or beside it. Langua strikes through errors and bolds fixes; Univerbal adds grammar tags ([Lingtuitive](https://lingtuitive.com/blog/best-ai-speaking-apps)). Pronunciation, where apps do it well, runs on a separate sound-level model, not on the transcript.

Three patterns are worth copying. Langua lets the learner **check the transcript before sending** and bin a bad recording; this is the cheapest protection against a speech error being corrected as a learner error. Langua also invites learners to say a missing word in their own language. With Spanish forced, an English word becomes Spanish-looking nonsense (inference), so Posta's retry without a forced language is a fair workaround. Typing is an equal input in Langua, Univerbal, Duolingo Roleplay and Memrise, and Posta already allows it.

The main complaint about AI tutor apps is leniency. A reviewer got a perfect score from Speak with reversed word order, and Pingo praised nonsense syllables; both reports come from a competitor ([LanguaTalk on Speak](https://languatalk.com/blog/speak-app-review/), [on Pingo](https://languatalk.com/blog/pingo-ai-review/)). Part of that is the same faithfulness problem: a model that prefers likely words hears the expected sentence (inference).

## Streaming saves about a second but swaps the model

**Option A: stream PCM to Scribe v2 Realtime.** Expo SDK 57's `expo-audio` has `useAudioStream`, which delivers raw PCM buffers, so no new native module is needed ([Expo Audio docs](https://docs.expo.dev/versions/latest/sdk/audio/)). An Edge Function mints a single-use token (it expires in 15 minutes), and the phone opens the WebSocket straight to ElevenLabs ([ElevenLabs client-side streaming](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-api/guides/how-to/speech-to-text/realtime/client-side-streaming)). Audio should not pass through Supabase: Edge Functions have a 2 s CPU limit and a 150–400 s wall clock ([Supabase limits](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/functions/limits)). With `commit_strategy=manual`, the stop tap sends the commit, and the final arrives about **0.12–0.14 s** later in independent tests. Against upload plus 0.6 s today, the saving is roughly 0.5–1.5 s (an estimate; the slow end is on weak networks).

The risks are specific. Realtime is a different model with weaker evidence on noisy Spanish. Partials only start **after about 2 s of audio**, so "Sí, dale" gets no live text at all ([ElevenLabs commit guide](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-api/guides/how-to/speech-to-text/realtime/transcripts-and-commit-strategies)). Realtime keyterms are capped at 50 terms of 20 characters, and the reference shows no temperature setting. Its next-word prediction can show words the learner has not said yet. The phone's real sample rate can differ from the one requested, and Bluetooth headsets force 8 or 16 kHz. Whether `useAudioStream` can run next to the file recorder is undocumented.

**Option B: stay on batch.** Faithfulness is at least a known baseline to measure, and the code is simple. The cost is the second per turn. Uploading while recording does not help much, since batch needs the whole file and a 64 kbps clip is only 40–120 kB (inference).

**Option C: hybrid.** Stream for speed and also send the .m4a to batch as the stored transcript, for about $0.0018 per 10 s line. The hard part is disagreement after the tutor has already replied. The usual answer is a confidence gate: trust realtime when its word logprobs are high, and wait for batch when they are low. No one has published how often the two Scribe models disagree.

**On-device first pass.** `expo-speech-recognition` gives free instant partials, but results differ between iOS and Android, there are no verbatim or keyterm controls, and Speak found its server model beat its on-device model by 45% WER. It fits only as an offline fallback. Speech-to-speech models are a poor fit too: their transcript is a side output that "may not match" the audio ([Soniox wiki](https://soniox.com/wiki/speech-to-speech-models-vs-pipelines)).

## Recommendation: test, stream with manual commit, add a doubt rule

Stay on ElevenLabs for now. No other provider has better faithfulness evidence, and Scribe already offers forced Spanish, verbatim output, manual commit, per-word logprobs and single-use tokens. Run the error-preservation test on today's batch setup before changing anything. If realtime scores close to batch on the same set, ship Option A with the .m4a kept as a fallback and a quality check. If realtime is clearly worse, ship Option C with the confidence gate. Only if both Scribe models fix too many errors, test Soniox (same model for batch and streaming, token confidence, cheapest) and AssemblyAI (self-serve zero retention).

Change the UX in four small ways, whatever the engine. Show the transcript as "what I heard" with a quick re-record and an edit option. Never put the expected answer in keyterms. For stress-only pairs (andas/andás, hablo/habló, esta/está), tell Claude the accent mark is not evidence and it must not correct on it alone. For low-confidence words, have Claude ask or stay quiet instead of correcting.

### The error-preservation test

Build 150–300 short lines in **matched pairs**: the correct sentence and the same sentence with one planted error. Cover person agreement ("yo tiene"), voseo against tuteo, stress-only pairs, gender, ser/estar, final -s, prepositions and infinitive for a finite verb. Record three tiers: an Argentine TTS voice reading the wrong lines (a lower bound; check by ear that the voice did not fix them), fluent speakers reading them with an English accent, and real beginner clips transcribed verbatim by a native speaker. The main metric is the **error-preservation rate**: the share of planted wrong words written as spoken, following WEPR. Report four more numbers next to it: the false-correction rate (wrong word replaced by the correct form), the invented-error rate on the correct twins, WER against the verbatim reference, and how often hidden errors had low confidence. Keep written accents in scoring for the stress-only group.

| Order | Test | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Paired set through today's batch setup, tiers 1 and 2 | Whether the correction card can be trusted now |
| 2 | Same set with no keyterms, scene words only, and voseo forms | Whether keyterms hide or invent errors |
| 3 | Same set through Scribe v2 Realtime with manual commit | Faithfulness gap, disagreement with batch, real time-to-final on a phone |
| 4 | Stress-only pairs alone | Whether any engine beats chance, and the "do not correct" rule |
| 5 | Silence, noise, English, and one-word lines under 2 s | Filters, the English retry, and the 2 s warm-up |
| 6 | Device checks: stream plus file recorder, Bluetooth, 200–300 ms tail after the stop tap | Whether Option A is buildable as planned |
| 7 | Soniox and AssemblyAI on the same set | Only if Scribe fails tests 1–3 |
| 8 | Tier 3 real beginner clips | Whether TTS scores predict real scores |

## Conclusion

The research changes the order of work. The known problem was a lost second; the larger problem is that the feature Posta sells, a correct correction, rests on a property no vendor measures and no leaderboard rewards. A few hundred paired sentences will tell Posta more about its speech stack than any public benchmark, and the set can be reused for every later change of model, keyterm list or prompt.

It also changes what "better speech-to-text" means here. Some contrasts, like "andas" and "andás" from a learner with flat stress, are not in the audio for any engine to find. The lasting fix is a product rule: show what was heard, let the learner fix it, and correct only what the audio can support. Longer term, the only method with repeated evidence of large gains is training on the app's own verbatim learner audio, so saving consented, hand-checked clips now keeps that door open.
