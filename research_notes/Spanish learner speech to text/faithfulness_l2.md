# ASR faithfulness on second-language learner speech (does STT preserve or silently fix learner errors?) — notes as of October 2026

Scope note: almost every measured study below is on L2 **English**. Only one measured study covers L2 **Spanish**, and it measures pronunciation/WER, not grammar-error preservation. No study was found on rioplatense/voseo learner speech. Each finding states its language.

## 1. Do modern ASR models normalize, autocorrect or hallucinate toward fluent/grammatical output on learner speech?

### Takeaway
Yes, and it is measured, not just suspected: in three independent L2-English studies Whisper "fixed" a large share of learner grammar errors (about a quarter to a third of the most frequent errors; nearly all noun-morphology mismatches in one study), and it was the worst offender among the systems compared even though it had the best WER. Models with no or weak language model (CTC without LM, or models fine-tuned on verbatim learner transcripts) preserve errors better; no published error-preservation measurement was found for gpt-4o-transcribe, Scribe or Deepgram.

### Cited Findings
- (L2 English, children, spontaneous, 85 h; ACL 2024) Michot et al. state that SOTA ASR "tend to correct the speakers' errors, which renders giving corrective feedback impossible" and introduce a metric, WEPR (word error rate computed only on words annotated as learner errors; lower is better). Scores: Whisper Large WER 0.25 / WEPR 0.47; Whisper Large-v3 WER 0.30 / WEPR 0.45; Whisper Medium 0.26 / 0.46; XLSR-1B CTC (no LM) WER 0.31 / WEPR 0.44; their learner-fine-tuned wav2vec2 CTC model ChaLL-300M WER 0.30 / WEPR 0.38. So roughly 45–47% of error-annotated words were not transcribed as spoken by Whisper, vs 38% for the fine-tuned CTC model. — [Michot et al. 2024, arXiv 2406.03235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03235)
- (Same study) On the 20 most frequent learner errors: Whisper-Large preserved 67.3%, XLSR-1B 72.3%, ChaLL-300M 82.7%. Whisper-Large actively corrected 26% of them (XLSR-1B 22.9%, ChaLL 15%). Example: learner says "have" for "has" — Whisper-Large rewrote it to "has" in 23.1% of cases and preserved it in 58.7%; ChaLL rewrote 1.5% and preserved 87.5%. "A majority of the most frequent unwanted error-corrections are deletions." Qualitative examples Whisper fixed: "for fly" → "to fly", "a" → "an", "do" → "is", German-pronounced "Lampe" → "lamp". — [Michot et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03235)
- (Same study) Authors' hypothesis and conclusion: CTC models used without an n-gram LM preserve speaker errors better out of the box than encoder-decoder models, whose decoder acts as a "powerful language model, which smooths out errors"; fine-tuning on learner data helps further. — [Michot et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03235)
- (L2 English, Japanese adults, spontaneous, 214 files, CEFR A2–B2) Koizumi: "all three systems tended to correct erroneous morphological forms in verbs and nouns, with Whisper showing the most prominent trend." Of words whose morphology differed between human and ASR transcript, the share that were the ASR correcting a learner error: nouns — Google 57.6% (76/132), Rev AI 55.8% (63/113), Whisper large-v3 98.9% (263/266); verbs — Google 51.1% (23/45), Rev AI 38.5% (20/52), Whisper 86.2% (75/87). Conclusion: ASR transcripts, "particularly Whisper, may not be suitable for error analyses involving morphological forms." Note the denominator is morphological mismatches, not all learner errors. — [Koizumi 2024, LET Chubu journal 35](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/letcj/35/0/35_15/_pdf)
- (Same study) Koizumi cites Bear et al. (2023): on non-native read English, errors in "third-person singular -s and verb complements tended to be corrected during transcription." — [Koizumi 2024](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/letcj/35/0/35_15/_pdf)
- (L2 English, 137 utterances with intentional grammar errors) Whisper transcribed what was actually spoken in 46% of utterances vs 84.7% for a Kaldi ASR with a custom LM that included the expected erroneous forms. Examples: "a late afternoon" → "the late afternoon"; "am" → "was". Authors: "asr systems have an inherent bias from the language model towards the grammatically correct text." — [Spoken Grammar Assessment Using LLM, arXiv 2410.01579](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01579v1)
- (L2 English) Cambridge ALTA: Whisper has "a tendency to skip disfluencies and hesitations in the output"; fine-tuning and soft-prompt tuning can "effectively alter the decoding behaviour of Whisper to generate the exact words spoken." — [Ma et al., arXiv 2307.09378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09378)
- (L2 English, Japanese) Whisper "inherently omitted disfluencies": pruning disfluencies removed only 504 words from Whisper's output vs 1,296 for Google and 1,203 for Rev AI. — [Koizumi 2024](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/letcj/35/0/35_15/_pdf)
- (L2 English, L2-ARCTIC spontaneous) Whisper large-v3 detected only 2.5% of "um/uh" fillers with no prompt and 40.8% with a disfluency-style initial prompt — still the lowest of five systems; and the prompt "often causes Whisper to hallucinate and include words or expressions that are not in the audio." — [McGuire 2025, arXiv 2503.06924](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06924)
- (Accented conversational English: India, Indonesia, Latin America; 6k utterances; Stimuler, Sept 2026) Filler recall: Parakeet 0.4–4.2%, Whisper 1.6–7.1%; zero-shot Qwen3-Omni-30B 82.5–93.0%; LoRA-fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Omni-3B 76–86% with WER 6–10% (baselines 13–21%). Authors: "Commercial ASR systems ... are optimised for aggregate WER and provide no mechanism to control this trade-off"; they note WER penalises a tense shift ("was"→"is") the same as a garbled name. — [Beyond WER, arXiv 2609.20828](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20828)
- (English/German, not learner speech) nyra labs: base Whisper disfluency F1 is 12% (English) / 10% (German); adding trained "mode tag" decoder tokens raises it to 53% / 79% with frozen weights and 91% / 94% after fine-tuning — i.e. the acoustic information is in the encoder, the decoder's style policy suppresses it. Also "roughly 60% of a model's reported WER [on AMI] is style mismatch, not recognition error." Spanish not shown. — [nyra labs, June 2026](https://nyra-labs.com/research/multilingual-style-control); paper [arXiv 2607.18934](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.18934); earlier model [CrisperWhisper](https://github.com/nyrahealth/CrisperWhisper)
- (English, aphasia vs control; not L2) "Careless Whisper": hallucinations disproportionately hit speakers with longer non-vocal durations (aphasia 41% non-vocal vs 15% control); 38% of hallucinations contained explicit harms. Long pauses and halting delivery are also typical of beginners. — [Koenecke et al. 2024, arXiv 2402.08021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08021)
- (OpenAI gpt-4o-transcribe family) Developer-reported bug (Jan 2026): on long pauses / noise / non-speech sounds, gpt-4o-mini-transcribe and gpt-4o-transcribe output the last sentence of the supplied prompt although nobody said it. No staff reply recorded. Anecdotal, not a controlled study. — [OpenAI community thread](https://community.openai.com/t/transcription-model-gpt-4o-mini-transcribe-prompt-leakage/1371126)
- (General) Hallucination literature defines the failure mode as "fluent and coherent transcriptions" that keep "syntactically and semantically plausible structure" while departing from the audio, arising "when models prioritize distributional patterns over fidelity to audio input"; WER does not capture it. — [Demystifying Hallucination in Speech Foundation Models, arXiv 2502.12414](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12414); [SHALLOW benchmark, arXiv 2510.16567](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16567)
- (Industry, English learners) Speak (Dec 2025): "ASR models are typically better at understanding what the sentence means" than at "transcribing what the actual speech is"; they pair ASR with a separate speech-to-IPA phonetic model plus forced alignment. — [Speak engineering blog, matching-v2](https://www.speak.com/blog/matching-v2)

### Inferences
- The "yo tiene → yo tengo" risk is real and should be assumed for any attention-encoder-decoder or LLM-decoder ASR until measured. The English analogue ("have" → "has", third-person -s) is exactly subject–verb agreement, and Whisper rewrote it about one time in four.
- Best WER and best error preservation are different rankings (Whisper won WER and lost WEPR in Michot; won lemma accuracy and lost morphology in Koizumi). Choosing an engine by public WER leaderboards selects against the property this app needs.
- Architecture trend from the evidence: CTC without external LM > Whisper for preservation, but the gap is moderate (44% vs 47% WEPR untuned) and CTC models pay for it with much worse WER. The big gain came from fine-tuning on verbatim learner transcripts (38%). Architecture alone does not solve it.
- LLM-decoder ASR is not automatically worse: an audio-LLM (Qwen-Omni) preserved fillers far better than Whisper/Parakeet. Faithfulness looks like a training-style / instruction property as much as an architecture property. But filler recall is not grammar-error preservation; the two were not measured together.
- Short single-line utterances remove some risks (long-form drift, condition-on-previous-text loops) but give the decoder very little acoustic context, so the language prior has more weight per word; a 4-word line with one wrong ending is the worst case for a strong LM.

### Gaps
- No published error-preservation measurement found for gpt-4o-transcribe / gpt-transcribe, ElevenLabs Scribe v2, Deepgram Nova-3, AssemblyAI Universal, Speechmatics, Gemini or Google Chirp on learner grammar errors. Vendors publish WER only.
- No study in any language other than English measures grammar-error preservation. Nothing for Spanish, nothing for voseo.
- No controlled comparison of CTC vs transducer vs AED vs LLM-decoder on the same learner-error set with current (2026) models; transducers (Parakeet-TDT) in particular were only measured on fillers.
- Bear et al. (2023) was only seen as cited by Koizumi; not read directly.

## 2. How accurate is ASR on non-native speech in general, and on L2 Spanish specifically? How much worse are beginners?

### Takeaway
Non-native speech roughly doubles error rates versus native on the same task, and the lowest-proficiency learners are roughly twice as bad again as advanced ones; clean read sentences from intermediate learners are near 5% error, spontaneous beginner speech is 17–30%+. The one L2 Spanish study gives 11–12% WER for English-L1 learners at B2–C1 reading 4–7 s sentences — beginners will be worse.

### Cited Findings
- (L2 Spanish, read, 4–7 s clips; 20 English-L1 British students B2–C1 from SPLLOC, plus Russian and German L1 groups) English-L1 learners: SeamlessM4T 11% WER / 5% CER; Whisper-large-v3 12% WER / 4% CER. Across the three L1 groups Whisper-large-v3 averaged 14% WER / 9% CER, SeamlessM4T 15.67% / 7.33%, Wav2Vec 20% WER / 6.3% CER. Substitutions were the most common error; models did better on female than male speech. Example errors on English-L1 speakers: "platos" → "plazos" (Whisper) / "brazos" (Seamless); "Javier" → "javi". — [Sarymsakova & Martín-Rodilla 2025, Phonica 21](https://revistes.ub.edu/index.php/phonica/article/download/49237/44105/148972)
- (Same study) Authors conclude ASR output alone is not adequate for L2 Spanish pronunciation feedback and "requires ... a detailed phonetic analysis"; the wav2vec CTC model had the lowest CER but highest WER (it writes what it hears, including non-words such as "eren" for "eran"). — [Sarymsakova & Martín-Rodilla 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394163431_Assessment_of_L2_Spanish_pronunciation_accuracy_via_Automatic_Speech_Recognition)
- (L2 English, read single sentences, L2-ARCTIC, 2,400 clips, 6 L1s) Mean match error rate: Whisper large-v3 0.054, AssemblyAI Universal-2 0.056; native US controls 0.007 across systems; Vietnamese-L1 0.143; Spanish-L1 speakers 0.049–0.083 depending on system. L1 had a large effect (ges 0.778). Spontaneous speech: best was Rev AI at 0.063. — [McGuire 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06924)
- (L2 English spontaneous, Japanese A2–B2, telephone-quality audio) Normalized WER non-native 20.7–23.8% vs native 11.9–13.8% on the same task. By level: A2 25.4%, B1 21.5%, B2 21.1% (proficiency not significant in their model). — [Koizumi 2024](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/letcj/35/0/35_15/_pdf)
- (L2 English, Speak & Improve corpus) Baseline Whisper-small gives nearly 17% WER on A2 speech and under 8% on C1; plain fine-tuning lowers average WER but "widens disparities and disproportionately harms lower-level learners"; proficiency-aware multitask training plus targeted augmentation cut WER up to 29.4% relative and insertion/deletion errors up to 58.6%. — [Sun, Zhu & Shi, arXiv 2510.10738](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10738)
- (L2 English) Speak & Improve Corpus 2025: about 315 h of learner audio with scores, subsets with manual transcripts and error annotation. Challenge baseline ASR WER 10.4% on dev (4.9% sub, 4.0% del, 1.5% ins); a team fine-tuning on L2 speech reached 4.2% on a dev subset vs 9.6% for the Whisper baseline. The challenge scores "speech WER" — what the speaker said, not a cleaned transcript. — [S&I Corpus, arXiv 2412.11986](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11986); [Challenge paper, arXiv 2412.11985](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.11985); [Challenge summary, ISCA SLaTE 2025](https://www.isca-archive.org/slate_2025/qian25_slate.pdf)
- (L2 English, Swiss children) WER 0.25–0.30 for Whisper Large / Large-v3 untuned on young learners' spontaneous speech. — [Michot et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03235)
- (Industry, English learners, June 2024) Speak: modern streaming ASR "often struggle to accurately transcribe heavily-accented speech from beginner non-native speakers"; their Conformer-CTC trained on thousands of hours of their own learner audio cut WER >60% vs the pre-trained baseline and beat Apple Speech and Azure (no absolute numbers given). — [Speak engineering blog, asr-levelup](https://www.speak.com/blog/asr-levelup)
- Corpora: SPLLOC is the main spoken L2 Spanish corpus (English-L1 British learners); L2-ARCTIC (L2 English read, 24 speakers, 6 L1s); Speak & Improve 2025 (L2 English, CEFR-graded). — [Sarymsakova & Martín-Rodilla 2025](https://revistes.ub.edu/index.php/phonica/article/download/49237/44105/148972); [McGuire 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06924); [Cambridge dataset page](https://researchdatasets.cambridge.org/datasets/speak-and-improve-corpus-2025)

### Inferences
- A realistic planning figure for English-L1 beginners (A1–A2) speaking short Spanish lines is one wrong word in every 5–8 words with a general-purpose engine, since B2–C1 learners already sit at 11–12% WER on read sentences and the A2-vs-C1 ratio in English is about 2x. This is an extrapolation, not a measurement.
- Low WER numbers hide the problem twice: (a) fixed errors count as "correct" if the reference was also cleaned, and (b) a 5% error rate concentrated on function words and endings is exactly where grammar feedback lives.
- Fine-tuning on own learner audio is the only approach with repeated evidence of large gains (Speak, Michot, S&I teams), but it can hurt the weakest learners unless handled deliberately.

### Gaps
- No WER figures found for L2 Spanish beginners (A1–A2), for spontaneous L2 Spanish, or for any commercial API (OpenAI, ElevenLabs, Deepgram, Google) on L2 Spanish.
- The Sarymsakova study is small (20 English-L1 speakers, read speech, Peninsular target) and tested only open models up to Whisper-large-v3.
- No data on Argentine-accent target norms: whether engines treat a learner's "vos tenés" differently from "tú tienes".

## 3. Spanish morphology-specific risks (verb endings, stress-only contrasts, gender agreement, s-aspiration, written accents, locale and keyterms)

### Takeaway
No source measures these for ASR; the evidence is indirect but points one way: the errors that matter for Spanish grammar feedback sit in short unstressed endings and in stress placement, which is where L2 English studies show the language prior overriding the audio, and English-L1 learners produce exactly the vowel and stress cues that Spanish models rely on least reliably.

### Cited Findings
- Stress alone distinguishes Spanish verb forms, e.g. hablo "I speak" vs habló "s/he spoke". — [Languages 8(4):258, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/languages8040258); see also [audiovisual stress perception study, PMC12426129](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12426129/)
- English speakers' perception of Spanish lexical stress is context-driven rather than purely acoustic (L2 stress is a known weak point for English-L1 learners). — [Journal of Phonetics study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0095447013000090)
- (L2 Spanish, English-L1) ASR errors arose where learners' vowel formants fell outside standard Spanish ranges and where the stressed-syllable vowel was lengthened/distorted; Whisper "failed to account for the duration of the accented vowel." — [Sarymsakova & Martín-Rodilla 2025](https://revistes.ub.edu/index.php/phonica/article/download/49237/44105/148972)
- L2 Spanish learners "often omit or use the wrong affix for nominal and verbal inflections in oral production," more than in writing. — [Montrul, SSLA 2011](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/morphological-errors-in-spanish-second-language-learners-and-heritage-speakers/5A47201A62AF07950D529CBAAB4EABF0)
- (English analogue) Morphological endings are the category ASR corrects most: third-person -s, plural -s, -ed; Whisper 86–99% of morphological mismatches were corrections. — [Koizumi 2024](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/letcj/35/0/35_15/_pdf)
- Keyterm/keyword features are designed to bias output toward listed terms: ElevenLabs — "highlight words or phrases to bias the model towards transcribing them", up to 1000 keyterms; Deepgram — up to 500 tokens, advised for jargon and names, and it says to avoid common words "rarely misrecognized"; Deepgram's own example shows confidence for a boosted phrase rising from 0.83 to 0.98. Deepgram's docs do not discuss false positives. — [ElevenLabs keyterm prompting](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-api/guides/how-to/speech-to-text/batch/keyterm-prompting); [Deepgram keyterm docs](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/keyterm)
- Deepgram Nova-3 offers Spanish as `es` and `es-419` (Latin America). — [Deepgram announcement](https://deepgram.com/learn/deepgram-expands-nova-3-with-spanish-french-and-portuguese-support)
- Whisper's initial prompt changes style but is not an instruction channel ("Whisper doesn't follow instructions like a general-purpose text model", 224-token limit), and style prompts raised hallucination in L2 tests. — [OpenAI speech-to-text guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/speech-to-text); [McGuire 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06924)

### Inferences
- Verb-ending confusions rank by acoustic distance. "tiene" vs "tengo" differ in a whole syllable and consonant, so if the transcript shows "tengo" after "yo" when the learner said "tiene", that is the language model, not the ear — this is the cleanest test item. "tenés" vs "tienes" differ in stress and diphthong; "hablo/habló", "andas/andás", "esta/está", "compro/compró" differ in stress only; a learner with flat or English-style stress gives the model almost no acoustic evidence, so the decoder will choose by context every time. For these, no ASR can be trusted and the honest output is "ambiguous".
- Written accents are produced by the decoder as part of spelling, so an accent in the transcript is evidence of what the model thinks the word is, not of where the learner put the stress. Showing "habló" to a learner who said "hablo" with odd stress (or the reverse) is a likely false-correction source.
- Gender agreement endings (-o/-a) are unstressed final vowels; English-L1 learners reduce them toward schwa, so "la problema" vs "el problema" or "buena/bueno" will often be decided by the LM in favour of the agreeing form — the same mechanism as English plural -s (98.9% in Koizumi).
- S-aspiration cuts both ways: a model tuned to Argentine/Latin American speech is used to missing final /s/ and restoring it from context, so it is also more willing to add an -s the learner did not say ("vos tené" → "vos tenés", "tú tiene" → "tú tienes").
- Voseo keyterms ("tenés", "querés", "sos", "podés") help if the engine otherwise rewrites correct voseo into tuteo ("tienes") — a normalisation in the other direction — but they bias the engine toward hearing voseo when the learner said "tienes" or "tiene". Vendor guidance says not to boost common words. Any keyterm list should be A/B tested on the error set before use; listing the expected answer's words is the strongest possible bias toward hiding the error.
- Forcing the language to Spanish is safe and useful (stops English/Portuguese mis-detection on short clips). A regional code is a weaker lever: it may shift spelling/vocabulary priors but nothing found shows that es-AR or es-419 changes voseo handling.

### Gaps
- No measurement found of ASR accuracy on Spanish stress-only minimal pairs, on written-accent correctness, on gender-ending preservation, or on voseo vs tuteo forms, for natives or learners. The search result on diacritic coverage was about Arabic, not Spanish, and is not used here.
- No evidence found on whether any vendor's es-AR / es-419 setting changes verb-form output.
- No vendor documents the false-positive rate of keyterm boosting.

## 4. Techniques for faithful, verbatim transcripts and for detecting uncertainty

### Takeaway
There is no switch that makes a general ASR preserve grammar errors — "verbatim" options only govern fillers and repetitions. What has evidence behind it is (a) models trained on verbatim learner transcripts, (b) a second, LM-free acoustic view (CTC/phoneme model plus forced alignment) to cross-check the words that matter, and (c) treating confidence scores as weak, miscalibrated hints rather than proof.

### Cited Findings
- Verbatim/disfluency controls exist but target fillers: ElevenLabs Scribe v2 is verbatim by default and has `no_verbatim` to remove "filler words, false starts and disfluencies"; Deepgram `filler_words=true` (Nova family); AssemblyAI `disfluencies` (false by default); Rev AI `remove_disfluencies`. None is documented as affecting grammar. — [ElevenLabs STT docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/speech-to-text); [Deepgram filler words](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/filler-words); [McGuire 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06924)
- Verbatim-trained Whisper variants: CrisperWhisper (verbatim "every filler, pause, stutter" or "intended" mode, word timestamps) and the 2026 mode-tag successor; shown for English and German. — [CrisperWhisper repo](https://github.com/nyrahealth/CrisperWhisper); [nyra labs 2026](https://nyra-labs.com/research/multilingual-style-control)
- Fine-tuning or soft-prompt tuning Whisper on verbatim learner transcripts changes its decoding to "generate the exact words spoken." — [Ma et al., arXiv 2307.09378](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09378)
- Fine-tuned wav2vec2-CTC on learner data gave the best error preservation (82.7% of top-20 errors vs Whisper 67.3%) with no LM in decoding. — [Michot et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03235)
- A custom LM that contains the expected wrong forms beat Whisper 84.7% vs 46% on utterances with planted grammar errors — i.e. when the task is constrained (known prompt), putting likely errors into the recogniser's vocabulary/grammar works. — [arXiv 2410.01579](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01579v1)
- Speak's production design: streaming ASR fine-tuned on non-native speech + a phonetic model that outputs IPA directly + forced alignment against the target line; reported "40% reduction in false negatives without making the algorithm more lenient." — [Speak, matching-v2](https://www.speak.com/blog/matching-v2)
- WhisperX-style pipelines force-align Whisper text to a wav2vec2 CTC phoneme model for timing, a ready-made way to get a per-word acoustic score. — [CrisperWhisper paper, Interspeech 2024](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2024/zusag24_interspeech.pdf)
- Phoneme-level wav2vec2-CTC is the standard backbone for mispronunciation detection and diagnosis in L2 speech. — [arXiv 2311.07037](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.07037); [arXiv 2203.15937](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.15937)
- Confidence is overconfident: Whisper assigns confidence above 0.7 to 10–20% of wrong tokens in noisy conditions; hallucinated segments often have high avg_logprob and low no_speech_prob and pass Whisper's built-in filter. Word confidence is the average of token log-probs. — [Overconfidence in Noisy ASR, arXiv 2509.07195](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.07195); [Whisper hallucination detection, arXiv 2606.07473](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07473)
- OpenAI transcription exposes a confidence option for the gpt-4o-transcribe family (reported as an include-logprobs option); the current guide lists gpt-transcribe (recommended), gpt-4o-transcribe, gpt-4o-mini-transcribe, whisper-1 (the only one with word timestamps) and a `keywords` parameter, and warns that when post-correcting with a text model you should "evaluate corrections against the original audio to avoid changing what the speaker said." — [OpenAI speech-to-text guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/speech-to-text)
- Prompts are a hallucination channel: prompt text leaked into gpt-4o-transcribe output on silence/noise (community report), and Whisper initial prompts raised hallucinations. — [OpenAI community thread](https://community.openai.com/t/transcription-model-gpt-4o-mini-transcribe-prompt-leakage/1371126); [McGuire 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.06924)
- Disabling `condition_on_previous_text` and adding VAD are the standard Whisper hallucination mitigations used in research setups. — [Koizumi 2024](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/letcj/35/0/35_15/_pdf)
- Cascaded spoken GEC (ASR → disfluency removal → GEC) "suffers from error propagation between stages"; S&I 2025 baseline feedback F0.5 was only 0.22. End-to-end Whisper-based SGEC needs lots of data (77 h → 2,500 h via pseudo-labels) and improves when low-confidence edits are filtered and edits caused by ASR errors are removed by reference alignment. — [End-to-End SGEC, arXiv 2506.18532](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18532); [S&I Challenge, arXiv 2412.11985](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.11985)
- Audio-LLMs: Qwen-Omni fine-tuned with LoRA produced both a verbatim and a corrected transcript in one pass (WER 6–10%, filler recall 76–86%); as judges of speech, GPT-4o-audio and Gemini 2.5 Pro reach moderate agreement with humans (Gemini average correlation 0.640); GPT-4o is reported more hallucination-resistant than other audio-LLMs but not immune. — [Beyond WER, arXiv 2609.20828](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20828); [Audio-aware LLMs as judges, EMNLP Findings 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.25.pdf); [GPT-4o voice mode exploration, arXiv 2502.09940](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.09940)
- Training-free hallucination mitigation for LLM-based ASR by re-ranking hypotheses with an acoustic likelihood exists as a 2026 research direction. — [arXiv 2608.30776](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.30776)

### Inferences
- Practical stack suggested by the evidence, in order of cost: (1) Spanish forced as language, no prompt, no keyterms, no previous-text conditioning, greedy/temperature 0, VAD-trim silence — this removes prompt bias and hallucination triggers but does not remove the decoder's own grammar prior. (2) Run a second engine of a different family (a CTC model without LM, e.g. a Spanish wav2vec2/XLS-R or Conformer-CTC) and flag any word where the two disagree; disagreement on a verb ending is the cue to show "not sure" instead of a correction. (3) For the specific words where an error is plausible, score both candidates ("tiene" vs "tengo") against the audio with a phoneme-CTC forced aligner and take the acoustically better one. (4) Longer term, fine-tune on the app's own verbatim-transcribed learner audio.
- Because app lines are short and the expected answer is usually known, the task can be turned from open transcription into a closed choice among the target sentence and a small generated set of likely learner errors (wrong person, tuteo form, wrong gender, infinitive). The Kaldi custom-LM result (84.7% vs 46%) is the evidence that this reframing works; it avoids both the LM-fixing problem and free hallucination. It must include the wrong forms, or it becomes the strongest "hide the error" bias.
- Temperature 0 and "no prompt" reduce variance and invention but cannot be expected to change the correct-the-learner behaviour, which is in the weights. No source tested this directly.
- Per-word confidence is useful mainly as a ranking signal for "which word to double-check"; a corrected word can be emitted with high confidence precisely because the LM likes it. Calibrate thresholds on the app's own error set.
- Asking a multimodal LLM to transcribe and correct in one call is the highest-risk design for this requirement: the model is rewarded for a coherent story (transcript + matching correction) and can invent an error or hide one, with nothing independent to check it. If used, it should receive the transcript from a separate verbatim-biased recogniser, or its transcript should be cross-checked the same way as any other engine.
- Lowering LM weight is only available in engines that expose it (hybrid/Kaldi-style, CTC with shallow fusion); hosted AED/LLM APIs do not expose it.

### Gaps
- No evidence found for or against temperature, beam size, or "transcribe exactly, do not fix grammar" instructions changing grammar-error preservation in gpt-4o-transcribe / gpt-transcribe / Gemini.
- Whether OpenAI's logprobs option is still offered on the newest gpt-transcribe model was not confirmed from the current guide (the fetched page did not list it).
- No study on two-engine disagreement as an error-masking detector, or on n-best lists for learner grammar errors; this is an engineering inference.
- No evaluation of Allosaurus or universal phone recognisers on L2 Spanish found; no Spanish-specific forced-alignment accuracy figures for learner speech.
- No public write-ups from Duolingo, ELSA or Loora on preserving grammar errors were found; ELSA/Duolingo material located was not specific enough to cite.

## 5. How to design a test (evaluation set, metrics, TTS vs real recordings)

### Takeaway
The published recipe is: transcribe learner audio verbatim, mark each learner error, and score the engine only on the marked words (error-preservation rate), alongside ordinary WER against the verbatim reference. Synthetic speech can screen engines cheaply but understates the problem, because real learners are disfluent and off-target in ways TTS is not.

### Cited Findings
- WEPR: annotate erroneous words in the verbatim reference (they used an "@!" tag), align hypothesis to reference at word level (they used a phonetic alignment), and compute (substitutions + deletions on annotated words) / (number of annotated words). Report next to WER, CER, chrF. Also report per-error confusion tables: for each frequent error, the share preserved vs the share rewritten to the correct form. — [Michot et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03235)
- Their text normalisation was customised (kept contractions); normalisation choices change results. — [Michot et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03235)
- Morphology-focused variant: among words matching at lemma level, count morphological mismatches and manually classify which ones are the engine correcting a learner error. — [Koizumi 2024](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/letcj/35/0/35_15/_pdf)
- Utterance-level variant: 137 utterances with intentional grammar errors, scored as exact recognition of what was spoken (46% vs 84.7%). — [arXiv 2410.01579](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01579v1)
- Score against what was said, not a cleaned transcript ("speech WER"), as the S&I Challenge does. — [S&I Challenge, arXiv 2412.11985](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.11985)
- Style mismatch can dominate WER (about 60% of reported WER on AMI), so references and hypotheses must share one convention for fillers, numbers, punctuation and accents. — [nyra labs 2026](https://nyra-labs.com/research/multilingual-style-control)
- WER treats a tense shift and a garbled name equally; task-specific recall metrics (entities, fillers) and an error taxonomy with an LLM judge validated on human labels (83.8% agreement on 210 samples) were needed. — [Beyond WER, arXiv 2609.20828](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20828)
- Synthetic L2 speech: "written and spoken L2 differ fundamentally: spontaneous speech includes disfluencies and discourse markers"; TTS plus voice cloning is useful as augmentation when texts are "speechified" and voices matched to proficiency — it is presented as a supplement to real data, not a replacement. — [Data Augmentation for L2 Speaking Assessment using TTS, arXiv 2607.10790](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10790)
- Standard TTS-evaluation via ASR "primarily measures speech intelligibility", i.e. TTS audio is built to be easy for recognisers. — [Evaluating Speech Synthesis by Training Recognizers on Synthetic Speech, arXiv 2310.00706](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.00706)
- Hallucination rises with pauses and non-vocal share. — [Koenecke et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08021)

### Inferences
- Suggested test set for this app: 150–300 short lines in matched pairs — the correct sentence and one planted error — covering the categories that matter: person agreement ("yo tiene"), voseo vs tuteo ("vos tienes", "tú tenés"), stress-only pairs (hablo/habló, andas/andás, esta/está), gender ("la problema", "un casa"), ser/estar, missing/extra final -s, wrong preposition, infinitive for finite verb. Pairs let you measure both directions: errors hidden (said wrong, transcribed right) and errors invented (said right, transcribed wrong).
- Metrics to report per engine and per category: error-preservation rate on the planted word; false-correction rate (planted error replaced by the correct form specifically, as opposed to any other mistake); invented-error rate on the correct twins; WER against verbatim reference; and, if confidence is available, how often hidden errors had low confidence (is the miss detectable?).
- Three audio tiers give the most information for the cost: (1) TTS in a native Argentine voice reading the wrong sentences — isolates the pure language-prior effect with perfect acoustics; an engine that fixes errors here will certainly fix them for learners; (2) proficient speakers deliberately reading the wrong sentences with an English accent; (3) real beginner recordings from the app, transcribed verbatim by a native speaker. Tier 1 is a lower bound on the problem: TTS has clean vowels, native stress and no hesitation, while the L2 Spanish study shows real learners' vowel and stress cues are what break recognition.
- TTS cannot represent the stress-only category properly unless the voice can be made to stress the wrong syllable on purpose, and TTS engines may themselves "fix" ungrammatical text prosodically; spot-check the audio by ear before trusting any score.
- Keep the scoring normalisation explicit about written accents: for the stress-only category accents must be kept in scoring; for everything else, score both with and without accents to separate spelling noise from real word changes.
- Run every configuration change (prompt, keyterms, locale, temperature) on the same set — the Whisper filler result (2.5% → 40.8% with a prompt, plus more hallucinations) shows settings move behaviour in both directions.

### Gaps
- No published evaluation set of Spanish learner utterances with annotated grammar errors and audio was found; SPLLOC has learner audio and transcripts but was not confirmed to carry word-level error annotation.
- No study quantifies how well TTS-based error-preservation scores predict scores on real learner audio.
- No guidance found on minimum sample sizes; Michot's per-error tables rely on the 20 most frequent errors in an 85-hour corpus.
